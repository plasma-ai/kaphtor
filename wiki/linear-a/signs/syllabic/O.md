### Forms cited for the value

- rare in Linear A—O-vowel problem supports non-Greek language: of 2,599 main-series syllabograms, 79 (3%) have the vowel o and 362 (14%) e; o prefers the last two syllables by more than 1.5:1, e by almost 2.5:1 (Davis 2026b, 159–60 and n. 605, 197)

### Note

Tomas finds o low but significant at Haghia Triada, RO often ending a word (Tomas 2003, 247, apud Salgarella 2020, 291 n. 6). Palaima and Sikkenga (1999) take Linear A, and by implication Minoan, for a three-vowel system, since Linear B added most o-signs; Davis (2014, 240–42), Melena (2014, 86) and Meissner and Steele (2017) object (Salgarella 2023, 411). O can stand alone, especially on roundels, at Khania only there, and is never part of a composite sign (Salgarella 2020, 156, 232). Davis now takes Minoan for the descendant of a three-vowel proto-language, a, i, u, with e and o secondary, some of them in the last two syllables from \[ai\] and \[au\] fused into '(usually long) \[ē\] and \[ō\]' when a suffix was added, a hypothesis he calls 'tentative' (2026b, 160–61, Hypothesis 19). Proto-Semitic has the same three vowels, and -ay contracts to -ē in A-TA-I-\*301-WA-E ʾatainawē, the Palaikastro spelling of -WA-JA, and in A-DE ʾAddē.
