### Note

We find U-DE-ZA 2 at HT 122a .1, the first entry after \]RA-RI ·, in a roster of men totalled KU-RO 31 (GORILA I 207), followed by HT 122b .3 \]A-RA-JU-U-DE-ZẠ 2, Ḥudeṯ of ʾArāyu (GORILA I 208–209). U writes ḥu with the guttural dropped, as A writes ḥa in A-JA ḥayyā and A-NA-NE ḥanān. DE writes de, and ZA the final ṯ, its vowel empty; the Z-series writes ṯ in ZU-DI ṯudī and KU-ZU-NI Kuṯinu as well.

Ḥudeṯ 'New Moon', the name of a boy born on the first of the month. Ugarit has the man ḥdṯ, syllabic ḫu-da-ši, and ḥdṯn, ḫu-ud!-ša-nu (Huehnergard 2008, 230), names DULAT sets under ḥdṯ 'new moon' as /ḥud(a)ṯ-/ (DULAT 351) and Fleming beside Emar ḫidaši 'new moon' (apud Watson 1996b, 98). Hebrew has the woman Ḥōdeš of 1 Chr 8:9, 'Born at the day of new moon', whom HALOT sets beside the two Ugarit names (HALOT 294). The Old Syrian glossary files the element in names at Ebla (u₉-du-sum), Mari (ḫu-du-šu-um) and Ugarit (GlOS 1, 232). Phoenician and Punic have BNḤDŠ 'son of the new moon' (Benz 1972, 308), which Greek calques as Νουμήνιος (Krahmalkov 2000, 106), and Ḥudis, god of the new moon (KAI 37; Krahmalkov 2000, 178). On Cyprus one such Eteocypriot inscription consists of a single name, pu-nu-to-so or bu-nu-do-šo, which Gordon reads as bn ḥdṯ, 'son of the new moon', a name 'appropriate for a boy born on the new moon' (Gordon 1963, 30).

The name may be the root of Odysseus—the hero who returns as one month wanes and the next rises (Od. 19.306–307; so Mosenkis 2025, 23)—and the Amorite form is its likeliest source. Mari's ḫu-du-šu-um, ḥuduṯ (GlOS 1, 232), is Ὀδυσσ- vowel for vowel, with ḥ lost, d as δ, and ṯ as the σσ of the epic and the ττ of Attic Ὀλυττεύς. Amorite names are widespread in Linear A: Qadmān is Amorite Qadmānum (see KA-DU-MA-NE), and Kubānu, Amorite Kubb-ān-um, stands in this same roster (see KU-PA₃-NU). In Hoekstra's words, 'the "Return of the Hero at New Moon" must have been a theme of the old saga' (Hoekstra 1989, 226, on Od. 14.457, apud Louden 2011, 293). Odysseus himself comes home on the feast of Apollo Neomenios, 'of the new moon' (Levaniouk 2011, ch. 11; Louden 2011, 292–93). Greek hears Minoan d as δ or λ, as dubur gives λαβύρινθος (see DU-PU₂-RE), hence Ὀλυσσεύς and Ulixes. The second vowel wavers in Greek as in Semitic (ḥudṯ, ḥudaṯ, ḥuduṯ), υ beside the ι of the Corinthian Ὀλισεύς. Rivals: ḥdṯ 'new'; a spelling of U-\*325-ZA (Montecchi 2010, 22). Two attestations, the two faces of one account.
