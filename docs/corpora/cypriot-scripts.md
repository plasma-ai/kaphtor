# Cypriot Scripts: Cypro-Minoan, the Cypriot Syllabary and Eteocypriot

Cypro-Minoan (CM) is the undeciphered syllabic script of Late Bronze Age Cyprus. Davis takes Linear A to be clearly
the "progenitor" of CM, though the means by which CM arose from it are still unclear (2026b, 431 n. 899). The Cypriot
syllabary (CS) of the first millennium came out of CM by reform (Egetmeyer 2013, 107–8) and writes Greek and
Eteocypriot (EC), a non-Greek language whose signs carry known values while its affiliation stays unidentified
(Steele 2013, 160). For Linear A the three serve as controls: CM signs whose shapes and values recur in Linear B and
the CS, a sister script's handling of its own languages, and a later language on the same island. None of them
confirms a Linear A reading.

## Editions and the edition of record

Inscriptions carry Olivier's ## numbers with a siglum for site, medium and serial: the eighth vessel from Kition is

states which edition governs each range (451):

| ADD##218–244 | Ferrara 2013, *CMI* II | no |

Appendix A gives each reading first as the corpus has it, then Valério's interpretation, the corrected parts set in
bold (451). A reading taken from it is therefore one of two things, the corpus reading or Valério's emendation,
and is cited as one or the other, never as the governing edition's.

In Appendix A, ¶ transcribes an inscribed sign that Valério takes as an entry or 'paragraph' marker. It follows the
last sequence of an entry, firmly so on the Ugarit tablet and only possibly on the cylinder (2016, 88, 158, 547).
Valério joins into one word a word that the edition carries over a line end with a hyphen (88, Table 2.4). Read that
way, a five-sign sequence that runs over the line end at .04–.05 of the cylinder ##097 ENKO Arou 001 stands again as
the whole text of the seal KOUR Psce 001, in the corpus reading and in Valério's emendation of one of its signs alike
(473, 503), and the second sequence of ENKO Abou 012, two signs long, lies whole inside a sequence of the cylinder's
lines .12–.13.

- ***HoChyMin*** (Olivier 2007, ##001–217). By Olivier's own account it falls short of a corpus (Perna 2024, 137–38;
  also Duhoux 2013, 27 n. 1, who finds too many inaccuracies in it). It misses about eighty inscriptions, most on
  vessels, keeps Masson's subscripts and adds "CM 0" (Donnelly 2024, 42).
- ***CMI* II** (Ferrara 2013), which Steele describes as editing 243 objects as a corpus, mostly without
  transcriptions (2013, 12). It gives photographs and drops the subscripts. It adds over thirty inscriptions, and
  about fifty remain unpublished or uncollected (Donnelly 2024, 43). Perna judges it unusable for epigraphic work, its
  drawings reproduced only roughly and two tablets printed upside down (2024, 138–39).
- **Neither is a corpus**, which is why Perna has taken one in hand (Egetmeyer 2024, 131).
- **Perna's corpus**: 245 inscriptions, CM 1 in one volume and CM 2 and 3 in the other (2024, 137, 139), announced
  for Incunabula Graeca (141). Perna's report numbers the inscriptions after *HoChyMin* (138 n. 5).
- **Single-sign texts** are excluded from both collections (Donnelly 2024, 42–43).

## One script or three

É. Masson split CM into CM 1, the main syllabary (85 signs), and CM 2 (59) and CM 3 (44), two branches Masson judged
very probably derived from CM 1 (1974, 11, 13–16). Donnelly's Table 2 sets out the ethnic reading attached to the
split: CM 1 "Linear/diverse" and local Cypriot, CM 2 "Squared" and Hurrian, CM 3 "Cuneiformized" and Ugaritian (2024,
39).

| Position | Source |
|---|---|
| Two scripts for two languages; CM 3 open | Olivier 2013, 11 (= 2024, 82–83): CM 1 and CM 2 are two scripts recording two languages, and on CM 3 the documentation is too scant for certainty. Olivier rejects Palaima's single-script critique (2024, 82). |
| CM 3 apart | Duhoux 2013, 39: CM 3 more probably stands as a script of its own than as a branch of either CM 1 or CM 2, a conclusion that awaits further data. The CM 1 and CM 2 differences are "impressive" (32). |
| One script, one language | Ferrara 2013, 75: there is no ground for supposing that Cypro-Minoan wrote more than one language, and, arguably, little ground for supposing it took more than one script. |
| Open; nothing forces several scripts | Valério 2016, 444: the evidence does not compel the theory of several scripts, and CM 2 has seven peculiar signs, not the sixteen or seventeen claimed before. |
| The specialists' current view | Davis 2026b, 29: Masson's hypothesis persists, while many CM specialists now treat the script as one writing system. |
| "CM1" as a category | Steele 2013, 47: the label suggests a unity that the group lacks. |
| The premise of the split | Donnelly 2024, 39: it takes for granted that differences of ethnicity reduce to differences of language. |

Davis's Hyp. 44 holds that CM1, CM2 and CM3 encode one language, or perhaps languages very closely related, or
dialects of a single language very like each other. The analyses, Davis states, leave it unproven, still a
hypothesis, but strong statistical data support it, enough that it ought to stand as the prevailing view of the
language behind the three subcorpora (2026b, 391).

The sign counts disagree with each other. Masson counts 114 in all (1974, 11), Duhoux 72, 61 and 50 for CM 1, 2 and 3
(2013, 28, 32, 39). Davis lists Olivier's 96, Ferrara's 74, Valério's 57 to 70 and Polig and Donnelly's 88 (2026b, 29
n. 125), where Donnelly 2024 gives that signary 89 (25). The paleographic studies meant to settle a "standard" list have
in fact widened the disagreement instead of narrowing it (Davis 2026b, 29). *HoChyMin* and Valério's Appendix A sort
by CM 1, 2 and 3, which Valério calls "the artificial divisions" (2016, 451); *CMI* II does without them. A subscript
label is a sorting key.

## Sign values

There is no agreement. Valério finds little agreement on what the script's signs are worth (2016, 27). Palaima judged
every scheme for deciphering Cypro-Minoan, past or current, improbable (1989a, 121), and Olivier endorses the verdict,
adding only that the situation is unchanged (2024, 87). Duhoux: readings and identifications have been put forward,
but none has yet won general acceptance, and Palaima's verdict, regrettably, still holds (2013, 29 n. 13). Donnelly:
comparison with Linear A and the Cypriot syllabary has made neither most Cypro-Minoan signs readable nor its language
or languages identifiable, and Valério's comparisons give readings for some thirty signs only, many of them still
hotly disputed (2024, 20).

The secure core is the handful of signs whose shape and value agree across Linear A or B and the CS:

- **Olivier:** a dozen signs alike in shape and in sound, which could only have arisen, in form and in value, from a
  Linear A ancestral both to Linear B and to the first-millennium syllabaries, to the latter by way of Cypro-Minoan
  (2024, 65, 79). They are no more than a sixth of CM 1's signs (84–85).
- **Steele:** the shared signs are about ten (2013, 49): 004, 005, 006, 007, 008, 013, 023, 044, 102, 104
  (Table 5, 51). Of these Steele is sure of the phonetic value of only three, pa (006), a (102) and se (044), and sure
  only to a limited degree, since the languages are unknown (78).
- **Donnelly:** nine to eleven signs have secure sound values, and these suggest at least four vowels in
  Cypro-Minoan, a, e, i and o, and the consonants -s, t/d, r/l and -p (2024, 20).
- **Egetmeyer:** working from the evidence as it is, one should take five CM signs to have approximately known
  phonetic values (2013, 122).
- **Valério:** nine values confirmed by all three of the thesis's procedures, CM 09 → li, 19/79 → u, 23 → ti,
  75 → ra, 85/96/114 → ri, 88/89/90 → jo, 97 → ro, CM 102 → a and CM 104 → i (2016, 441). The full grid is
  Table 5.27 (442). Valério's count of proposed values varies: 60 sign forms in the abstract, 50 sign values at 446.

**Who proposed what.** Valério's Appendix D gathers the CM sign values that comparative analyses proposed from 1953 to
2013 (2016, 652–56), nine scholars' values side by side: Sittig, É. Masson, Saporetti, Faucounau, Nahm, Duhoux,
Olivier, Facchetti et al. and Steele. Report each value with its proposer's own marks. CM 23, for example (653):

| Sittig | É. Masson | Saporetti | Faucounau | Nahm | Duhoux | Olivier | Facchetti et al. | Steele |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| b/p/phi | ṭV, ti(e) | mo?, ti? | d/ti | ti | ti? | ti | ti | ti |

Donnelly judges it likely that 023 has the value *ti*, since the CS *ti* sign matches Linear A's *ti* in shape and
value (2024, 21). Valério's own three grids (Tables 3.126, 4.21, 5.27) stand beside
Appendix D, and Faucounau published a grid of values for CM2 (1980, 379, fig. 1). Faucounau numbers the signs by
Masson's general table (1977, 226), and each value stays under the number Faucounau gives it. The grid agrees with
Valério's report of that column wherever the two number a sign alike (23 *d/ti*). At 69 they differ by a letter:
Valério prints 69b *mo* (2016, 655) where the grid has 69c *mo*, and neither the 1977 nor the 1980 article prints a
69b. Counted along Masson's row 69 (1974, 14, fig. 3), the grid's 69c and 69d are its two CM 2 drawings. Valério's
*š/sa* under 82 goes back to the 1977 figure, which sets *sa* at 82 and at 57 (Faucounau 1977, 237, fig. 1), while
the 1980 grid has 57 alone. At 110 the numbering accounts for nothing: Faucounau sets the sign as a second *a*,
written a₂ (1980, 380), where Valério's cell reads *k/ḫe, za* (2016, 656), and Donnelly's concordance keeps 110 one
number in Masson, Olivier and Ferrara (2021, 276). Valério removes a question mark for each method that agrees
(2016, 303): a bare value has all three behind it, "?" two, "??" one (441).

**08 and 13.** Valério's grid sets 08 at *na* and 13/78 at *to* (2016, 442); Steele gives 013 a value "na or
similar" and pairs 008 with *to* (2013, 54, 57). Valério traces the confusion of the two signs to *HoChyMin*'s
renumbering of É. Masson's forms (2016, 108–109). A value stays under the number its source prints.

**From CS values to CM signs.** Davis draws a corollary from Hyp. 50: CS sign values can tentatively be transferred
to their CM homomorphs (2026b, 432), a method that may prove far more productive and valuable than earlier work
supposed (433). Davis prints no value for any CM sign, so a value drawn from the corollary is derived, not Davis's.
Donnelly finds it unclear whether Cypro-Syllabic took over Cypro-Minoan's sound values beyond a handful of cases
(2024, 20). For Egetmeyer, how far CM values match the CS ones is still unsettled (2013, 122).

## The language or languages

Davis states that the language CM encodes is unknown (2026b, 27). Steele holds full decipherment impossible on the
present evidence (2013, 79). A Semitic, an Anatolian or a Hurro-Urartian language in some Cypro-Minoan inscriptions is
not in itself impossible, nor even improbable, but the decipherer bears the burden of proof (14–15).

Three texts or sets of texts run past 100 signs and draw the language proposals: the cylinder ##097 ENKO Arou 001, the
Ugarit tablet ##215 RASH Atab 004 and the Enkomi tablets ##207–209 ENKO Atab 002–004. The same proposals recur, an
indigenous language before Eteocypriot, Semitic at Ugarit and Hurrian at Enkomi, but no proposed reading or language
identification has been applied to several inscriptions and given consistent positive results, that is, grammar
identified (Donnelly 2024, 27).

Of name readings in general, Olivier warns that reading a personal name as Greek in one text and as Ugaritic, even as
Hurrian, in another at best 'confirms' the values of the signs that share their shapes with the first-millennium
syllabaries, and that in texts this short the argument may simply run in a "vicious circle" (2024, 85). On CM 1
against CM 2, Duhoux judges different languages more likely than one and the same (2013, 38).

**Davis 2026b, Hyps. 48–50.** Both of Davis's main experiments, A and B, indicate:

- Hyp. 48: Greek is not CM's language (431).
- Hyp. 49: the languages of LA and CM differ (431).
- Hyp. 50: EC and CM encode a single language, or perhaps two languages very closely related, two dialects of one
  language very like each other, or one language at two chronological stages (432).

Hyps. 48 and 49 rest on a null result. For the LB and LA texts set against CM, Davis finds no grounds to reject H₀,
that each score arises from chance alone, and draws from this strong statistical evidence that LA and CM, though
related as scripts, are not related in their languages (431). A score that chance explains fails to show that two
languages are related; it does not show that they differ, so both hypotheses claim more than the test measures, and
Hyp. 49 is the one that bears on Linear A (this guide's analysis). Hyp. 50 stands on a positive result: the EC score is
"statistically incompatible" with chance (431–32).

Davis states that the analyses leave Hyp. 50 unproven, still a hypothesis, though strong statistical data support it,
enough for it to be taken as the prevailing view of a linguistic link between EC and CM (433). Of all the
hypotheses, Davis asks readers to take them for what they are, hypotheses and not proof of what they propose (436).

## Eteocypriot

Eteocypriot is a modern term, one Johannes Friedrich coined in 1932, for the language of those Cypriot syllabic
inscriptions whose non-Greek linguistic features can be demonstrated (Steele 2013, 101). An inscription counts as
Eteocypriot only when identifiable Eteocypriot features are detected in it (106).

**Corpus.** Steele counts twenty-six possible inscriptions, some more securely included than others (2013, 118–19).
EC 1–18 come from Amathus or are assumed to; the rest come from Paphos (EC 19–22), perhaps Kourion (EC 23), and
Abydos (EC 25) and Karnak (EC 26) in Egypt (119–20). The Paphian texts date to the late sixth century or earlier,
EC 21 to c. 700 (119). Valério counts no more than twenty-five (2016, 397). Olivier's estimate of about seventy uses
the term broadly, for non-Greek languages in the syllabary (2013, 20). IG XV 1 labels 27 parts in 26 records
Eteocypriot, from Amathus, Kourion and Marion; set against Steele's list, 13 of Steele's numbers fall among them, 4
stand in IG under another label (EC 6, 7, 10 and 14, titled "syllabisch" there), 2 match no IG record securely, and 7
stand outside IG XV 1: six from other sites and EC 24, whose provenance is unknown, so that an Amathus origin cannot be
ruled out (Steele 2013, 119).

**The *-o-ti* ending.** Steele finds -o-ti closing twenty distinct Eteocypriot words, and twice within a word, and,
since known nouns carry it, takes it as strongly suggesting a case function of some kind, while a genitive function
is "very tentative" (2013, 135). Donnelly takes *-Co-ti* as genitive or dative, identified from the
Greek–Eteocypriot bilinguals (2024, 20–21). Duhoux compares CM sequence-final -23 (*ti*?) with EC *-ti* and
concludes that the two probably form an 'isogloss', and that CM 1 *-ti?* very probably marks a function like a
genitive singular (Duhoux 2009b, 65, 69, as reported by Valério 2016, 397). Valério sees in CM an opposition of -ø or -o
(nominative?) against -o-ti (genitive?), directly matched in the Eteocypriot of the next millennium (2016, 446).
Donnelly allows that the ending was shared between two languages through their proximity, by areal diffusion (2024,
21).

**The Amathus bilingual, ICS 196** (Egetmeyer 2010, II, Amathus 7). For Steele this inscription by itself suffices to
show that the Eteocypriot language existed (2013, 105). Its Greek is a dedication by the Amathusians' city to
Aristōn, Aristōnax's son, called well-born (105). The EC text is longer than the Greek (Steele 2013, 170; Davis
2026b, 33). Egetmeyer lists two to four EC–Greek texts from Amathus, each digraphic with alphabetic koine Greek:
ICS 196, 196d, 196e and Hermary et al. 1989 (2010, I, 18–19).

**Affiliation.** In Steele's judgement it seems that, as things are now known, no language meets every one of the
four criteria laid out in the book, and for Hurrian the evidence falls short of demonstrating affiliation (2013,
160). EC has been set beside Anatolian, Semitic, Hurrian/Urartian and even Illyrian, and no convincing link with any
of them has ever been shown (Davis 2026b, 31 n. 130, after O. Masson 2007, 245). Gordon read the Amathus text as
Semitic; those readings are kept with its control record in this repository (see Layers and commands).

**Continuity with CM.**

## From Cypro-Minoan to the Cypriot syllabary

- **Reform.** Egetmeyer describes CM as replaced, a direct link tying it to the script that followed, which must be
  the product of reforming Cypro-Minoan (2013, 107–8), and the borrowing is supposed to have gone from CM 1 into CG
  (123). The CS has 55 signs (Donnelly 2024, 20).
- **Date.** A new script based on CM emerged in the 11th c. BCE (Davis 2026b, 31). For Olivier, a plausible history of
  the first-millennium syllabary requires that it was created at Paphos and dates from the eighth century, if not
  slightly before (2013, 20). Egetmeyer prefers a lower date for the script reform, since the CG syllabary's lack of
  labiovelars tells against a high one (2013, 130).
- **The Opheltas spit (##170).** Olivier classes it as still Cypro-Minoan (2013, 16; 2024, 87), a classification
  Egetmeyer accepts (2013, 130). Duhoux assigns it to one of the Cypriot syllabaries other than Cypro-Minoan (2013, 27
  n. 2).
- **Evidence.** Steele finds that almost nothing shows how the scripts fared in this period (2013, 18).

## Cypro-Minoan and Linear A

- **Descent.** Olivier holds beyond doubt that the Cypriot syllabaries came from Crete (2024, 79). Steele leaves open
  whether CM descends directly from Linear A or both descend from a common ancestor (2013, 50). Steele and Meissner:
  if the oldest Cypro-Minoan inscriptions known fall within 100–200 years of the arrival of writing in Cyprus, Linear
  A is the one well-attested candidate for their direct ancestor, though no definite decision is possible (2017, ms.
  6–7). Donnelly: transfer from Linear A is almost certainly assured, though how it
  happened and how far it went are disputed, and Linear A may have been one source among several (2024, 9). Linear A
  supplies twelve to thirty of CM's roughly ninety signs (20).
- **"CM 0", ##001 ENKO Atab 001.** Dikaios dated its level to c. 1525–1425, and because it resembles Linear A more
  closely than Cypro-Minoan, Olivier sets it between the two, past Linear A and not yet Cypro-Minoan (2024, 81–82).
  Olivier coined the label in *HoChyMin* (2013, 9 n. 6). For Duhoux the evidence suggests that CM 0 did not give rise
  to the other, later CM syllabaries known (2013, 30). Steele: almost certainly bound up with Linear A in some way
  (2013, 48).
- **Where transfer happened.** Donnelly favours the Cyclades over Crete, though no one item of evidence settles it
  alone (2026, 61). Of the Melos jug handle MI Zb 3, doubtful (D 9) in RILA-S1, Donnelly judges it best read as
  CM 023-082, a Cypro-Minoan sequence, and, if that reading is accepted, takes the handle as the sole direct
  archaeological tie linking Cypro-Minoan with Linear A (2026, 71–72). The Kalo Chorafi loom weight, probably Cretan
  in origin and given a proposed date of MM III–LM I, bears a Cypro-Minoan sign made before firing (Egetmeyer 2024,
  126), a sign Donnelly calls certainly not Linear A, yet not indisputably Cypro-Minoan either (2024, 9).
- **Linear B.** The two were contemporaries, and nothing shows that either script influenced the other (Donnelly
  2024, 9).

## Outside Cyprus

- **Ugarit:** nine inscriptions, and a tenth (SYRI Psce 001) reportedly acquired in Latakia (Valério 2016, 62).
  Masson's CM 3 had 8 documents (Olivier 2024, 82); Perna assigns twelve to CM 3 (2024, 137).
- **Tiryns:** three documents (Valério 2016, 62), among them a clay ball probably of the post-palatial period (Steele
  2013, 45).
- **Ashkelon:** one ostracon and 18 jar handles (Cross and Stager 2006, 129), proposed as "the Old Philistine script"
  (134). Davis counsels skepticism, for now, toward any claim that Philistine inscriptions use Cypro-Minoan or a script
  directly related to it (2011, 62). Egetmeyer's verdict on the script is non liquet (2024, 126), and Valério
  excludes the ostracon from the corpus (2016, 60). Donnelly lists Ashkelon among the findspots of CM handle
  inscriptions (2026, 72).

## Layers and commands

- **Cypro-Minoan catalogue** (`data/ane/cypro-minoan/`): the 263 Cypro-Minoan texts keyed by siglum, the 254 of
  Valério's Appendix A and nine witnesses outside it. Each record gives the ## number, the site and object type decoded
  from Valério's key to the sigla (2016, 36–37), Pleiades and Trismegistos ids as identity controls only, the edition
  of record, and metadata statements, each with work and page: museum numbers, identities, editions, findspots, dates,
  dimensions, Donnelly's document forms (2021, 260–271), hands and visual evidence. No transcription ships: Valério's
  Appendix A is a doctoral thesis whose terms allow private research and teaching use only. A statement whose text is
  the source's own sentence ships as a pointer (field, work, page).
- **Query the catalogue:**

  ```bash
  uv run --no-sync python tools/linear_a/cypro_minoan_lookup.py --doc "ENKO Atab 004"
  uv run --no-sync python tools/linear_a/cypro_minoan_lookup.py --site KITI --type Avas
  ```

  `--doc` prints one text, found by siglum, ## number, museum or object number. `--site` and `--type` take Valério's
  site and type codes and list the texts that match (KITI Avas: Kition's vessels). `--format json` prints either as
  JSON.

- **IG XV 1** (`data/ane/ig-xv-1-cypriot/`, CC BY 4.0): the Cypriot syllabic inscriptions of *Inscriptiones Graecae*
  XV 1, Cypriot Greek and Eteocypriot, with their editions, places and dates.
  `uv run --no-sync python -m tools.ane.aneq cypriot Amathus` matches designation, title, place and edition, and
  `--place` restricts by find place.
- **The Eteocypriot control:** the Amathus bilingual stands as `ETEOCYPRIOTE_AMATHUS_CONTROL` in the Eteocretan file,
  `data/linear-a/eteocretan_inscriptions.json`, with Gordon's readings and the relation `eteocypriote_control_only`:
  `uv run --no-sync python tools/linear_a/eteocretan_lookup.py --inscription ETEOCYPRIOTE_AMATHUS_CONTROL`.
  `tools/linear_a/comparative_corpus.py` names the same control `eteocypriot-control` and searches it only with
  `--include-excluded` (`--search Amathus --corpus eteocypriot-control --include-excluded`). The registry it reads,
  `data/linear-a/comparative_corpora/`, is not shipped, so in this checkout the command stops.
- **Not shipped:** the Cypro-Minoan transcription, its sequences and the signary, with the values each proposer gives,
  are not in this repository, nor are the open Sign2Vec and PASP layers that
  [aegean-comparative-corpora.md](aegean-comparative-corpora.md) describes. In six records a Sign2Vec sign stands
  against Appendix A's, ##212 and ##215 among them, both drawn by É. Masson (1974, 26, 33), and in 52 more Sign2Vec
  leaves out signs it judged damaged: at ADD##247 it drops the first sign, which Appendix A prints.
- **Unicode:** the Cypro-Minoan block, U+12F90–U+12FFF, encodes 99 characters, CYPRO-MINOAN SIGN CM001 to CM302.
  Nineteen Olivier numbers up to 114 have no code point: 003, 014, 016, 018, 020, 022, 031, 032, 042, 043, 045, 048,
  057, 065, 077, 093, 106, 111, 113.
- Nothing here is merged into the Linear A corpus (`data/linear-a/corpus.json`) or promoted into its readings.
